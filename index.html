Galaxy rotation and the missing mass problem

The outer stars and gas of a galaxy orbit faster than the gravity of its visible matter can explain. This site measures that gap in real galaxies and tests two explanations for it.

01

Background: from prediction to problem

What a rotation curve shows, what Newton’s gravity predicts, and what telescopes found.

  1. Definition

    A rotation curve is a speed chart

    Plot how fast a galaxy’s stars and gas orbit against their distance from the centre. Orbit speed works like a weighing scale: the faster something orbits at a given distance, the more mass lies inside its orbit.

  2. Prediction

    Outer stars should slow down

    Gravity weakens with distance. Mercury orbits the Sun at 47 km/s, Neptune at 5 km/s (Williams 2024). Most of a galaxy’s visible matter sits near its centre, so its outer stars should slow down in the same way: the dashed line.

  3. Observation

    They don’t

    By 1980 Vera Rubin and colleagues had shown that the speeds stay high, galaxy after galaxy (Rubin, Ford & Thonnard 1980). At the edge of NGC 3198, gas orbits at –, but its visible matter could only hold it at about –. Keeping it in orbit takes about – times the pull. That gap is the missing mass problem.

    How are speeds that far out measured?

    From the Doppler shift: gas moving towards us looks slightly bluer, gas moving away slightly redder. Cold hydrogen glows faintly at 21 cm in radio, and it reaches far beyond the stars, so the speeds go further out than starlight does (Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert 2016).

  4. Hypotheses

    Two possible answers

    Either galaxies hold far more mass than we can see, or gravity stops following Newton’s law when it is very weak.

    Dark matterThe galaxy sits inside a huge halo of unseen mass.
    MONDVery weak gravity is stronger than Newton’s law predicts (Milgrom 1983).

What is a dark matter halo?

Dark matter gives out no light, so we only detect it through its gravity. A halo is a huge, roughly round cloud of it around a galaxy, reaching far beyond the stars.

Show more: the NFW shape and a known weakness

Newton’s gravity stays as it is; there is just more mass. The simulator uses an NFW halo (Navarro, Frenk & White 1996). Its shape came out of computer simulations of dark matter clumping as the Universe grew, not from fitting rotation curves: dense in the middle, thinning outwards. Two sliders set it: mass (M200) and scale radius (rs, how spread out it is).

A known weakness. In many small, faint galaxies the inner speeds rise more gently than NFW’s dense centre allows: the cusp–core problem (de Blok 2010). Whether exploding stars could stir the dark matter enough to flatten it is still debated.

Show the maths (A-level circular motion)

1. Orbits. Gravity supplies the centripetal acceleration. For a round distribution of mass, only the mass M(r) inside the orbit pulls overall (Newton’s shell theorem):

v2r=GM(r)r2⇒v=GM(r)r

2. Visible matter alone. Beyond most of the stars, M(r) hardly grows, so v ∝ 1/√r: the speed should fall, like the planets’ speeds in the Solar System.

3. What a flat curve needs. For v to stay constant, M(r) must grow in step with r. That happens if the density falls as 1/r²: each thin shell of thickness Δr then holds the same mass, 4πr²ρΔr, so every step outwards adds the same amount.

4. The NFW density. With ρs setting how dense the halo is:

ρ(r)=ρsrrs(1+rrs)2

Well inside rs the density falls as 1/r; far outside, as 1/r³. Around rs it falls roughly as 1/r², just what step 3 needs, so over that range the halo’s speed stays nearly level. It peaks at about 2.2 rs, then falls only slowly (Navarro, Frenk & White 1996). Adding up the shells gives the mass formula used in the simulator (see Method).

What is MOND?

MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics) says there is no dark matter. Instead, gravity behaves differently when it is extremely weak (Milgrom 1983).

Show more: how big MOND’s boost gets

MOND boosts gravity everywhere, by an amount that depends on how strong it already is. Near a galaxy’s centre gravity is strong and the boost is small; far out it is weak and the boost is large, so the outer stars keep moving fast.

a0 sets the scale. MOND’s constant, a0 = 1.2×10⁻¹⁰ m/s², is about 10¹¹ times weaker than gravity at Earth’s surface. It is a turning point, not a switch. With the simulator’s formula, a star speeds up by:

Where does a0 come from?

a0 is measured from real galaxies, not predicted. It marks the middle of MOND’s change from normal gravity to boosted gravity, not where the boost starts.

Why is gravity already boosted at a0? MOND has two rules: for strong gravity, leave it alone (×1); for weak gravity, multiply it by √(a0 ÷ gN). The two rules give the same answer at a0. MOND’s formula blends them smoothly instead of switching sharply, so at a0 gravity is 1.6 times Newton’s. That is the +26% speed in the table above (√1.6 ≈ 1.26). Other blending formulas give 1.3 to 1.6 times (Famaey & McGaugh 2012).

What a0 controls. Far out, MOND says a galaxy’s speed depends only on its visible mass M and a0: v⁴ = G M a0 (Milgrom 1983). Measure v and M, and you can work out a0.

Find a0 yourself

Each dot is one point in a real galaxy. Across: how strong its visible matter’s pull is. Up: how many times stronger gravity must be there to match the measured speed. The dots are measurements, so they stay put. Slide a0 to move MOND’s curve until it runs through them.

The measured value. 153 SPARC galaxies give a0 = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s², the same for every galaxy, just as MOND claims (McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016).

A strange coincidence. a0 is close to c × H0 ÷ 2π, built from the speed of light and the Universe’s expansion rate (Famaey & McGaugh 2012). No one knows why.

Show the maths (A-level circular motion)

1. Orbits. Gravity supplies the centripetal acceleration, and Newton gives the pull gN of the visible mass M:

g=v2r,gN=GMr2

2. MOND’s rule. Strong gravity is almost unchanged; weak gravity is boosted:

g=gN if gN≫a0
g=gNa0 if gN≪a0

3. Far out, put the weak rule into step 1:

v2r=GMa0r
⇒v4=GMa0

r cancels, so the speed stops falling: a flat rotation curve set by the visible mass alone (Milgrom 1983). Between the two limits the simulator blends smoothly, using a formula fitted to SPARC galaxies (McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016; see Method).

    What is a mass-to-light ratio?

    Telescopes measure a galaxy's light, not its mass. The mass-to-light ratio (M/L) converts one into the other: Suns' worth of mass per Sun's worth of light.

    • Low M/L means young stars. Young, massive blue stars are very bright for their mass.
    • High M/L means old stars. Old populations are mostly small, dim red stars: lots of mass, not much light.

    SPARC’s standard values (3.6 μm infrared) are 0.5 for the disk and 0.7 for the older bulge: averages, so each galaxy differs a little. A higher M/L lifts the visible-matter curve and leaves less for dark matter.

    Best realistic fits may scale them by 0.6 to 1.6, the range stellar models allow.

    Source: Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert (2016), SPARC; McGaugh & Schombert (2014).

    What is the tilt?

    We see every galaxy at an angle. The tilt (or inclination, i) is that angle: 0° is face-on, 90° is edge-on.

    • Telescopes only see motion towards or away from us (the Doppler shift), not sideways.
    • So a tilted disk looks slower than it really spins. True speed = measured speed ÷ sin i.

    SPARC’s speeds are already corrected, but its tilt has an error of a few degrees. Nearly face-on galaxies are the most sensitive.

    Best realistic fits may change the tilt within twice SPARC’s error.

    Source: Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert (2016), SPARC; Li et al. (2018).

      What do χ² and χ2r mean?

      At each point, divide the gap between model and measurement by the error bar and square it; χ² is the total. χ2r divides that by the number of points minus the values tuned, so galaxies and models compare fairly. Missing every point by one error bar gives about 1; by three, about 9.

      Why not 0?

      Measurements are noisy, so even a correct model misses by about one error bar.

      Why count tuned values?

      More dials can always bend a model closer to the data. With standard values the halo tunes 2 (mass and size) and MOND none, or 1 once you move its a0 slider. Best realistic fits also tune M/L, distance and tilt (and MOND’s a0): 4 to 6 in total.

      Before drawing conclusions

      • A fair testEvery model starts from the same stars and gas, with SPARC’s mass-to-light ratio, distance and tilt. Best realistic fits let each model adjust those, and the score counts every value tuned, so extra freedom costs something.
      • Only realistic valuesStar masses that stellar models allow (McGaugh & Schombert 2014), distance and tilt within twice SPARC’s errors, and a typical halo concentration (Dutton & Macciò 2014). at limit means a better fit needed an unrealistic value. All limits
      • MOND’s constantBest fits may nudge a0 between 1.16 and 1.24 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s² (McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016). MOND says a0 is universal (Milgrom 1983), so galaxies needing clearly different values would be a warning sign.
      • Why MOND scores badly herePlain MOND tunes nothing, and some error bars are tiny (under 1 km/s across much of UGC 128), so a small steady miss adds up. With softer limits, Li et al. (2018) found acceptable MOND fits for most of SPARC’s 175 galaxies. A poor score here reflects these galaxies and limits, not a verdict on MOND.
      • What the error bars coverOnly each speed’s own uncertainty. A wrong distance or tilt shifts the whole curve at once, so the best fits vary those separately.
      • A small sampleA few of SPARC’s 175 galaxies (Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert 2016): enough to show the method, not to pick a winner.

      Beyond rotation curves: the wider evidence

      Rotation curves are one clue among several. A winning explanation has to account for all of them.

      Points to dark matter

      Galaxy clusters

      Seen: galaxies in the Coma cluster move too fast for the cluster’s visible mass to hold them together (Zwicky 1933).

      Why it matters: the first hint of missing mass. MOND shrinks the gap but still needs some unseen matter (Famaey & McGaugh 2012).

      Points to dark matter

      The Bullet Cluster

      Seen: two clusters collided. Their hot gas, most of the visible matter, slowed down in the middle, but the bending of background light shows most of the mass carried on with the galaxies (Clowe et al. 2006).

      Why it matters: the mass and the visible matter ended up in different places.

      Points to dark matter

      The oldest light

      Seen: the cosmic microwave background, light left over from the early Universe, has a pattern of slightly hotter and cooler patches.

      Why it matters: the pattern fits a universe with about five times as much dark matter as ordinary matter (Planck Collaboration 2020).

      Points to MOND

      Visible matter predicts the pull

      Seen: across 153 galaxies, the pull worked out from the measured speeds closely follows one curve set only by the visible matter (McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016).

      Why it matters: MOND predicts exactly this. Dark matter can match it only if every halo is closely tied to its galaxy’s visible matter, which still needs explaining.

      Still open: each idea’s unsolved problem

      No confirmed dark matter particle

      Decades of searches have found none (Bertone & Hooper 2018). In September 2026 the LZ experiment reported one unexplained event, but at 2.6σ it is far below the 5σ needed to claim a discovery, and it is not yet peer reviewed (LZ Collaboration 2026).

      MOND beyond galaxies

      MOND has not matched galaxy clusters or the cosmic microwave background without adding unseen matter of its own (Famaey & McGaugh 2012).

          1

          Start from real measurements

          Every speed on the Simulator comes from SPARC, a public catalogue of 175 disk galaxies (Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert 2016).

          For each galaxy, SPARC picks a series of distances from the centre, r, and lists at each one:

          • The measured orbit speed, Vobs, with its error bar, ±σ: the one-standard-deviation uncertainty in that speed.
          • The speed each visible part would give on its own: the gas (Vgas), the disk of stars (Vdisk) and the central bulge (Vbulge). SPARC works these out from radio maps of the hydrogen gas and infrared (3.6 μm) images of the starlight from the Spitzer Space Telescope.

          The site reads SPARC’s table as published for NGC 3198, NGC 2403, NGC 7331 and UGC 128; no speed is typed in or adjusted. (The Custom Sandbox galaxy is imaginary, so it has none.)

          Correcting for tilt

          A telescope only sees motion towards or away from us (the Doppler shift), so a tilted disk looks slower than it really spins. SPARC divides by sin i, where the tilt i is 0° face-on and 90° edge-on:

          Vobs=Vline of sightsini
          What if SPARC’s tilt is slightly off?

          SPARC’s tilt, iSPARC, has an error of a few degrees. If the true tilt is i, every speed changes by the same factor (Li et al. 2018, eq. 5):

          s=siniSPARCsini

          Example: SPARC says 60° but the truth is 55°: s = 0.866 / 0.819 ≈ 1.06, so the real speeds are 6% higher. At 30° instead of 25°, s ≈ 1.18. Nearly face-on galaxies are far more sensitive.

          With Best realistic fits on, each model may change i by up to 2× SPARC’s error (never past 90°). The page leaves the points where SPARC put them and divides the model by s instead, which gives the same score.

          2

          Add up the visible matter

          How fast things would orbit if only the visible stars and gas pulled: the cyan dashed line.

          Why add squares? For a circular orbit, the pull is g = V²/r, so V² = gr. The pulls of the gas, disk and bulge add up, so their V² values add too. The square root at the end turns the total back into a speed.

          Turning starlight into mass. Telescopes measure starlight, not mass. The two numbers in the formula are mass-to-light ratios: Suns’ worth of mass per Sun’s worth of infrared light. SPARC’s standard 0.50 (disk) and 0.70 (older bulge) come from models of how ageing stars change in light and mass (McGaugh & Schombert 2014). In use now: standard SPARC values.

          Vbar=Vgas|Vgas|+0.50Vdisk2+0.70Vbulge2

          Vbar is the speed a star would have if only the visible matter pulled on it.

          Why multiply by the mass-to-light ratio?

          SPARC gives Vdisk and Vbulge for a ratio of 1. V² is proportional to mass, so doubling the ratio doubles the stars’ mass and doubles their V².

          Why Vgas|Vgas| instead of Vgas²?

          Much of a galaxy’s gas lies in a ring further out. Inside a flat ring, the near side pulls harder than the far side, so near the centre the gas pulls outwards overall and SPARC lists Vgas as negative. (Newton’s shell theorem, that only the mass inside you pulls, holds for a round cloud but not for a flat disk.) Vgas|Vgas| keeps the minus sign that squaring would lose, so there the gas lowers the total.

          In the Custom Sandbox you set the masses yourself, so nothing needs converting from light. The stars and the gas are flat disks that fade steadily outwards (exponential disks), and the bulge is a ball of stars (a Hernquist profile).

          Their speeds add as squares, for the same reason as in a real galaxy: V² = gr, and pulls add.

          Vbar=Vdisk2+Vbulge2+Vgas2

          The thin dotted lines on the rotation curve show each part on its own.

          3

          Supply the missing pull

          The measured speeds sit above the cyan line, so something must pull harder. Each idea gives a different answer: the orange line.

          Answer A: a dark matter halo

          Keep Newton’s gravity and add unseen mass. The halo’s speed is worked out like any orbit, from the mass inside it, then added to the visible matter’s as squares:

          Vmodel=Vbar2+Vhalo2 Vhalo=GMhalo(r)r

          Mhalo(r) is the dark matter inside radius r. For a round cloud, only that mass pulls overall (Newton’s shell theorem). The two halo sliders set the halo’s mass, M200, and its scale radius, rs.

          The NFW mass formula Mhalo(r)=M200f(r/rs)f(c) f(x)=ln(1+x)−x1+x,c=r200rs

          f describes how the mass builds up with radius in an NFW halo, the profile that cosmological N-body simulations of cold dark matter produce (Navarro, Frenk & White 1996). Dividing by f(c) makes the mass inside r200 exactly M200.

          r200 is the halo’s conventional edge: inside it, the average density is 200 times the Universe’s critical density, the density that would make the Universe’s geometry exactly flat (with H0 = 70 km/s/Mpc, about five hydrogen atoms’ worth per cubic metre). The model adds no dark matter beyond it.

          Answer B: MOND

          Keep only the visible matter and change the law of gravity instead (Milgrom 1983). Three steps, with a0 = 1.2×10⁻¹⁰ m/s²:

          1. Newton’s pull from the visible matter:gN=Vbar2r
          2. MOND’s boost:g=gN1−e−gN/a0
          3. Back to a speed, since g = V²/r:Vmodel=gr

          What the boost does. When gN is much bigger than a0, the bottom of the fraction is almost 1, so gravity is plain Newton. When gN is much smaller, writing x = √(gN/a0), 1 − e−x ≈ x, which gives g ≈ √(gNa0): a stronger pull that keeps the curve flat.

          Why this formula?

          MOND fixes how gravity behaves when it is strong and when it is very weak, but not the blend in between. This site uses the radial acceleration relation’s formula (McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016, eq. 4), because a0 was measured with it from the same SPARC data, and Li et al. (2018), whose fit limits the site follows, use it too. Older work often used the “standard” formula, μ(x) = x/√(1+x²) (Famaey & McGaugh 2012).

          The choice matters for single galaxies. Plain MOND’s χ2r with each formula:

          GalaxyStandardRAR (used)

          So MOND’s formula was itself fitted to SPARC (one curve shared by 153 galaxies, not tuned to each), while the halo is fitted to each galaxy separately. Neither model meets this data completely fresh.

          4

          Score the fit

          How far each orange line misses the measured points, with their error bars as the ruler.

          For each point, find the gap between the model and the measurement, divide it by that point’s error bar and square it. Add these up to get χ² (“chi-squared”). Squaring means misses above and below both count, and big misses count far more than small ones.

          Then divide by N − k, the number of points minus the number of values tuned to fit this galaxy. The result, χ2r, is roughly the squared miss per point. About 1 means the curve misses by about one error bar: as close as the measurements allow.

          Why subtract k? A model with more dials can always be bent closer to the data, so each dial counts against it. With standard values, k = 0 for visible matter only and for MOND at the measured a0 (1 if you set a0 yourself), and 2 for the halo (its mass and scale radius, even when set by hand). Best realistic fits tune more: k = 5 (6 for NGC 7331) for the halo and 4 (5 for NGC 7331) for MOND.

          Close, rough or poor. The fit badge says Close fit below 2, Rough fit from 2 to 10 and Poor fit above 10. These cut-offs are this site’s own rule of thumb, not a published standard.

          Where the limits come from

          Mass-to-light: 0.1 dex of scatter around SPARC’s standard values (McGaugh & Schombert 2014; Li et al. 2018). Distance and tilt: SPARC’s own errors. Concentration: the scatter of simulated halos (Dutton & Macciò 2014).

          a0: measured as 1.20 ± 0.02 (McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016). They also quote a wider ±0.24, but that comes from the stars’ mass-to-light ratio, which the fits already vary, so using it would count the same uncertainty twice.

          Distance matters because a galaxy twice as far away as thought must be twice as wide and four times as bright to look the same, so every radius and every visible-matter V² changes with it.

          5

          Watch stars move under that gravity

          The 3D view moves test stars under the model’s gravity for a billion years.

          Test stars feel the model’s gravity but do not pull on each other. There are 800 for NGC 3198, scaled with visible mass (200 to 2400): a display choice, since a real galaxy has billions.

          They start in balance. Disk stars start on circular orbits at the measured speeds; bulge stars get random motions (a velocity dispersion) that hold the bulge up, much as pressure holds up a gas. With too little pull they drift outwards, and past 1.5 × the visible edge they count as ejected.

          The pull on each star gives it an acceleration a = Vmodel(r)²/r towards the centre.

          One small step at a time

          An orbit cannot be solved in one go, so the computer moves each star in small steps of Δt = 0.5 Myr, 2000 times over 1 Gyr (the velocity-Verlet method). First the position, as in SUVAT’s s = ut + ½at²:

          xnew=x+vΔt+12aΔt2

          Then it finds the acceleration at the new position, anew, and updates the velocity with the average of the old and new accelerations:

          vnew=v+a+anew2Δt
          Why this method?

          Using only the old acceleration (Euler’s method) makes each step slightly wrong in a way that builds up, so the orbit’s energy drifts and stars slowly spiral in or out. Velocity-Verlet is symplectic: its energy error stays small and bounded instead of growing, so a star that leaves does so because of the physics, not the size of the step.

          !

          What this model leaves out

          The simplifications that matter most, so you can judge the results yourself.

          • A round pullThe 3D view treats the galaxy’s mass as a ball, though the disk is flat. The rotation curve uses SPARC’s own disk calculations and is unaffected; the stars’ bobbing is approximate.
          • A softened centreNear the centre the pull is smoothed over 0.5 to 1 kpc and is exactly Newtonian beyond 2.8 softening lengths (the GADGET-2 spline; Springel 2005), so close passes don’t fling stars out.
          • A smooth, round haloThe halo is perfectly smooth and stops at r200. In many small, faint galaxies, the speeds near the centre suggest a flatter middle than NFW’s (the cusp–core problem; de Blok 2010).
          • Estimated star massesMass-to-light ratios are averages, not measured per galaxy. Best realistic fits vary them within limits.
          • Hard limitsBest fits stop sharply at each range’s edge. With softer limits, Li et al. (2018) found acceptable MOND fits for most SPARC galaxies.
          • Four galaxiesSPARC has 175. Four show the method; they cannot decide between the ideas.
          • Drawn, not calculatedThe glow, star spikes, purple halo cloud and MOND’s gold shading are visual aids. They change no numbers.
          • Spiral arms are drawn onThe test stars don’t pull on each other, so they can’t form arms. The drawn arms follow density-wave theory (Lin & Shu 1964): a slower pattern the stars pass through, lit mostly by young stars and absent in gas-poor disks. Their shape and speed are a visual choice.
